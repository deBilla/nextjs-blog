Hi Guys, back in 2019 at the University of Peradeniya, Rajitha Rajasooriya, Kavin Ranawella, Dr. Namal Karunarathne and I wrote a paper called *Landing a Quadcopter on to a moving landing target using Computer Vision*. I reread it recently and noticed that some of the equations don't do what the text says they do. So I rebuilt the whole thing in **ROS 2**, wrote a simulator to test it, fixed the equations and measured the difference. In this post I'll go through why the paper exists, what was wrong, how the ROS 2 version works, and what the simulation says. There's an interactive replay in the middle so you can watch it land.

## Why we wrote the paper

Landing a quadcopter on the ground is easy. Landing it on a **moving** vehicle is not. The drone has to find the target, work out how fast it's moving and in which direction, cope with wind, and still touch down on a small pad.

A lot of earlier work solved this by letting the drone and the vehicle talk to each other, for example by sending the vehicle's GPS position over a radio link. In the real world you can't always count on that link. So the idea of the paper was to use only what the drone carries:

- one downward-facing camera
- cheap hardware (a Raspberry Pi for the vision and an Arduino Nano for flight control)
- a landing pad with **three ArUco markers of different sizes**

The three markers are the clever part. A big marker is readable from high up, a medium one for most of the flight, and a small one when the drone is almost on the pad. That way there is always one marker fully in view.

The plan in the paper has four steps: design the pad, detect the markers, track the target, and land. For the landing, the drone gets close to the pad, cuts its rotors and drops onto it. If the drop would miss, it aborts and climbs again.

## What OpenCV gives you

OpenCV's ArUco module finds the markers and solves for their pose. For each frame you get two vectors:

- `tvec = (tx, ty, tz)`, the position of the marker in the **camera's** coordinate frame, in metres
- `rvec`, the marker's rotation as an axis-angle vector

Everything in Section III-C of the paper is built from these two vectors.

## What was wrong with the equations

Here are the equations as we printed them, and what I replaced them with.

| Quantity | Paper | Problem | Replaced with |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Rotation | `rvecs = Σ aᵢ / (1 + x)` | Doesn't mean anything. `rvec` comes straight out of `solvePnP` | `R = cv2.Rodrigues(rvec)` |
| Distance moved | `√((tx₁² + ty₁²) − (tx₂² + ty₂²))` | Not a distance, see below | `‖p₂ − p₁‖` in a level world frame |
| Speed | `distance / duration` | Inherits the above, and amplifies frame noise | A Kalman filter on the pad's position |
| Direction | `angle = tyᵢ / txᵢ` | A ratio, and it's *where* the pad is, not where it's going | `atan2(vy, vx)` from the filtered velocity |
| Altitude | `tz` | Only true if the camera is perfectly level | `tvec` rotated by the drone's attitude |

The distance one is the most interesting. `tx² + ty²` is the squared distance from the pad to the camera's centre line. Subtracting two of those is not the distance the pad moved. Here are three simple cases:

- If the pad moves **away** from the centre line, the number under the square root is negative, so the "distance" is imaginary.
- If the pad moves in a circle around the centre line, the formula says it moved zero metres.
- If the pad moves 0.1 m towards the centre line, the formula says 0.44 m when it was 1 m off-centre, and 0.89 m when it was 4 m off-centre.

The correct version is the plain straight-line distance between the two positions:

```python
d = np.linalg.norm(p2[:2] - p1[:2])   # p1, p2 are pad positions in the world frame
```

The altitude problem is less obvious but matters more in flight. A quadcopter has to **tilt** to move sideways, and the camera is bolted to the frame, so it tilts too. At 4 m up, a 20° tilt makes the pad look about 1.5 m away from where it really is. The fix is to rotate the camera measurement by the drone's attitude (which the flight controller already knows) before using it. This is the core of the corrected estimator:

```python
# Camera axes in the body frame (FLU), for a camera looking straight down
R_BODY_CAM = np.array([[0, -1, 0], [-1, 0, 0], [0, 0, -1]])

def pad_position_in_world(tvec, drone_pos, R_world_body, cam_offset):
    """Where the pad really is, given one camera measurement and the drone's pose."""
    p_rel_body = R_BODY_CAM @ tvec + cam_offset
    return drone_pos + R_world_body @ p_rel_body
```

The pad's world position then goes into a constant-velocity Kalman filter. Its velocity estimate gives the pad's speed and direction, and the relative velocity is just `v_pad − v_drone`.

One last thing. In the paper's Table I, the speeds our system measured were close to what a phone speedometer on the vehicle reported. The formula as printed can't produce that (you'll see why in the first experiment), so I'm fairly sure the code we ran used a normal distance and the printed equation is a typesetting slip. Either way, the printed version needed fixing.

## Rebuilding it in ROS 2

I split the system into ROS 2 (Jazzy) nodes, one per job:

```text
 sim_world ──camera image──▶ aruco_detector ──pad pose──▶ target_estimator
     ▲   └────────── drone state (attitude, velocity) ───────▶      │
     │                                                              ▼
     └──────── velocity command, motor cut ◀──────── landing_controller
```

- **sim_world** simulates the drone, the vehicle and the wind, and **renders the downward camera image**. It doesn't use Gazebo. The scene is just three flat textured planes (ground, vehicle deck and pad), so each frame is three perspective warps in OpenCV. That's enough to give the real ArUco detector real images, with tilt, noise, blur and background clutter.
- **aruco_detector** finds the markers and estimates the pad's pose, using all visible markers together.
- **target_estimator** runs four estimators side by side on every detection: the **paper's** equations as printed, a **naive** fix (correct algebra, still in the camera frame), a **tilt-compensated** version, and the full **corrected** version with the Kalman filter. A parameter picks which one actually flies the drone.
- **landing_controller** is a state machine: SEARCH, TRACK, DESCEND, FINAL, CUT, LANDED, plus ABORT. In FINAL it hovers just above the pad and only cuts the motors when the predicted drop point is within 12 cm of the pad centre, which is the paper's projectile idea with a safety check.

Everything runs headless in Docker, so it works on a Mac without a GPU.

## Experiment 1: measuring the pad's speed

This repeats the paper's Table I experiment. The drone hovers 3.5 m above the deck and the vehicle drives underneath at 1 to 9 m/s. Every estimator sees exactly the same camera images.

![Reported speed against the true speed of the pad from 1 to 9 m/s. The paper's equation reports between 9 and 21 m/s, while the naive, tilt-compensated and corrected estimators all follow the truth line closely](./images/landing-a-quadcopter-on-a-moving-target-revisiting-my-2019-paper/1.png)

The printed formula reports 9 to 21 m/s for a pad doing 1 to 9 m/s, and it's undefined in about 47% of frames, every time the pad moves away from the centre of the image. The corrected estimator stays within 0.07 to 0.14 m/s of the truth at every speed. The naive and tilt-compensated versions are close on average but noisy, with errors around 0.5 to 1 m/s, because they divide frame-to-frame differences by 33 ms.

## Experiment 2: what tilt does

Next I took the 28 flights flown by the corrected estimator (7 vehicle motions, 4 random seeds each, about 22,000 detections) and compared every estimator's idea of where the pad is with the true position, grouped by how much the drone was tilted when the frame was taken. All four estimators saw the same frames.

![Median position error against drone tilt on a log scale. The camera-frame position used by the paper grows from about 3 cm when level to over 1.5 m at 25 degrees of tilt, while the tilt-compensated and corrected versions stay between 1 and 3 cm](./images/landing-a-quadcopter-on-a-moving-target-revisiting-my-2019-paper/2.png)

When the drone is level, the paper's camera-frame position is fine (about 3 cm off). At 10 to 15° of tilt it's 70 cm off, and above 20° it's over 1.5 m off. Rotating by the attitude keeps it between 1 and 3 cm at any tilt. The same goes for altitude: using `tz` directly was off by 11 cm on average, against 2 cm after the rotation.

The corrected (filtered) position is about a centimetre worse than the raw compensated one, because the filter smooths and lags slightly. In return its speed error is half as big (0.07 m/s against 0.15 m/s), and the speed is what the drop decision depends on.

## Experiment 3: does it actually land?

Each estimator flew the drone in 7 scenarios (stationary, straight at 2 m/s, accelerating, a circle, a lane-change weave, stop-and-go traffic and a fast 4.5 m/s run) with 4 random seeds each. A run counts as a landing only if the drone ends up on the pad, within 35 cm of the centre, hitting at under 2.2 m/s.

| Estimator flying the drone | Landings | What happened otherwise |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Paper's equations | 0 / 28 | Lost the pad in every moving scenario |
| Naive fix | 0 / 28 | Lost the pad, same reason (tilt) |
| Tilt-compensated | 6 / 28 | Mostly timed out after aborting |
| Corrected | 7 / 28 | Mostly timed out after aborting |

When the corrected version did land, it was 4 to 14 cm from the centre on average. So the estimation part works. What doesn't work yet is my **landing controller** on harder motions. It got 3 of 4 on the stationary pad and 2 of 4 on the straight one, but none on the weave, stop-and-go and fast runs. It gets down to the final hover, the pad shifts or brakes, the camera loses it, and it aborts and climbs. That's a control problem, not a vision one, and it's what I'll work on next.

## A lesson about the pad

The three-marker pad does what we wanted. Between 1.2 m and 4 m the pad was detected in 86 to 90% of frames. Below 0.5 m, though, the large and medium markers are almost always cut off and the small one does all the work.

The paper never said how big the markers should be. My first try used a 10 cm centre marker, and the camera lost it in the last 30 cm unless the drone was within 3 cm of the centre. At that height a 70° camera only sees about 42 × 31 cm of the pad. I ended up with 32, 20 and 16 cm markers, and even then the final half second before the drop runs on the Kalman filter's prediction. We wrote about this problem in the paper too: when the drone is very close, it can't see the target any more.

## Wrapping up

- The **motivation** still holds: landing on a moving vehicle with only a camera, without trusting a radio link.
- Three of the paper's equations were wrong (distance, angle, altitude) and one was meaningless (`rvecs`). The biggest effect in flight comes from **ignoring the camera's tilt**, not from the algebra.
- With the fixes, estimation is accurate to a few centimetres and about 0.1 m/s. Closed-loop landing works on steady targets but still needs a better controller for weaving and braking vehicles.

Next I want to improve the final approach so it copes with vehicles that turn and brake, and then try it on real hardware again.
